# Kezhul

> The red bell rings beneath the ice. The lake has no bottom to answer.

## The First Deposition

The bell was heard before the lake froze. It rang once beneath a fisherman's boat, and every fish in his net turned toward the shore. He cut the ropes. The fish remained suspended in their former positions until the boat drifted out of sight.

By morning, the lake was covered with ice the color of clear wine. Beneath it hung a red bell, large enough to contain the boat. Its rope rose through the frozen surface and continued upward beyond the clouds.

The fisherman tied a white ribbon around the rope. He returned after sunset and found the ribbon beneath the ice, knotted around the bell's clapper. The knot was his own, though he could not remember learning to tie it.

He never fished that water again. His boat continued to appear offshore without him.

## A Keeper Appointed by Reflection

The keeper was chosen when her reflection failed to leave the lake. She had stood on the bank at noon and walked home before dusk. The reflection remained, holding a little brass cup that she had never possessed.

The villagers brought her food and built a shelter beside the shore. They asked her to watch the reflection until it agreed to return. She accepted because the shelter's window faced the place where the bell's rope disappeared into the sky.

Each morning she placed an empty cup on the sill. Each evening it contained a small quantity of red dust. She poured the dust into a jar and marked the jar with a single line.

The lines eventually formed a ladder. Inside the jar, something began to climb.

## The Boat Under the Bell

The fisherman's boat appeared beneath the ice during the second winter. It rested upside down below the bell, its oars arranged across its hull like folded limbs. A figure sat inside the overturned vessel without falling out.

The keeper recognized the brass cup in its hands. Her reflection on the bank lowered its head. For the first time, the red bell rang while she was watching.

The sound entered the shelter through the window and left through a crack beneath the floor. Where it passed, frost formed into little horns. The keeper collected one in her palm. Inside it she saw a staircase descending through red light.

She placed the horn in the jar. The climbing thing stopped. Above the lake, the rope grew slack for a moment, then tightened around an invisible weight.

## What the Lake Concealed

The water had gathered inside the folded wing of a dragon whose body lay deeper than any sounding line. The ice was a memory of its eyelid. The red bell had been cast from the sound it made before its first sleep.

The keeper learned this from the figure in the boat. It spoke without lifting its head, and its words appeared as cracks in the ice. She read each crack by walking along it, never reaching the place where two lines met.

The dragon possessed no name that could remain whole above water. The villagers had tried several. Each became a fish, a strip of white cloth, or a bitter taste that lingered on the tongue.

The keeper tried none. She asked instead what the rope was holding. Beneath the boat, a vast pale claw opened around an empty space shaped like a standing person.

## The Clapper's Account

The white ribbon carried four dark marks:

*Kez — hul — ar — ven.*

The keeper copied them onto the shelter's window. Her reflection on the bank raised its brass cup. The red dust inside the jar became clear, and the ladder's highest rung extended through the lid.

Up it came a small figure made entirely of reflected light. It stepped onto the sill, entered the cup, and looked toward the lake.

Below the ice, the figure in the overturned boat finally lifted its head. It had the keeper's face, but its eyes were closed. The empty space within the dragon's claw became slightly warmer, and the rope cast a shadow across a sky without sun.

The keeper erased one mark. The bell rang twice. She could not remember which mark she had removed.

## The Winter Left Open

At dawn, the jar stood empty. The ladder remained on its glass, but its rungs now pointed downward. Outside, the keeper's reflection had vanished from the bank. In its place stood the fisherman, holding the white ribbon between two bare hands.

He asked whether his boat had returned. She pointed beneath the ice. There was no boat, only a red bell and a small brass cup resting where its clapper should have been.

The fisherman left the ribbon on her windowsill. Neither spoke of the figure who had been sitting inside the hull.

The lake still freezes red each winter. Its rope continues beyond the clouds. The shelter remains occupied, though no smoke rises from its chimney.

On the quietest nights, something beneath the ice tests the weight of an empty hand. The bell does not answer until the hand has closed.
